Rejected? Read the reason before you change anything

A rejection cites a specific cause, and the cause decides your next move. Changing the wrong thing costs another fortnight.

Get started Production access requirements

Sort the reason into one of three buckets

Almost every rejection lands in one of these categories, and they need opposite responses.

  • The test itself. Not enough opted-in testers, a window that was not continuous, or a test that did not run for the full period.
  • The app. A policy problem with the app, a broken experience, missing functionality, or content that does not match what the listing claims.
  • The paperwork. The application answers described no real testing, or the listing itself is incomplete.

The distinction that matters most

If the reason mentions testing, no amount of app polish fixes it. If the reason mentions the app or its policies, no amount of extra testers fixes it. Match the fix to the stated cause, not to the change that feels easiest.

If the reason was the test

  1. Audit who is genuinely opted in right now, rather than who is on the list.
  2. Get the count comfortably above twelve.
  3. Write down the date it holds. That becomes day one.
  4. Run the full fourteen continuous days, checking daily and replacing anyone inactive.
  5. Reapply with the record in hand, and reference the dates in your answers.

If the reason was the app

Fix the specific thing named, then check whether that fix needs a fresh test window before you reapply. A material change to the app during or after a test is worth accounting for in your answers rather than glossing over.

If the reason was the paperwork

Reapply with answers that describe a test only you could have run: the dates, the tester count, the devices, the feedback, and the changes you made. Generic answers about improving the user experience read as though no test happened.

What not to do

  • Do not add testers to a finished window. It does not extend or repair a completed period.
  • Do not buy headcount. Inactive testers and emulated devices are the fastest way to turn one rejection into two.
  • Do not resubmit unchanged. An identical application after a rejection wastes the review slot.
  • Do not guess at the cause. If the wording is unclear, ask Google through the console support route rather than assuming.

Running the window again, properly

The second attempt is usually easier, because you know where it broke. The requirement is unchanged: at least twelve opted-in testers, held for fourteen continuous days, followed by an application that describes what actually happened.
