What a managed closed test should include

You are not buying installs. You are buying twelve people who stay opted in for fourteen continuous days, with the record to prove it. Here is what that has to contain to be worth paying for.

The six things that have to be true

If a service cannot answer all six of these plainly, it is not selling you a closed test. It is selling you a headcount.

  • Twelve or more real testers. Physical Android devices, real Google accounts with a Play history. Not emulators, not freshly created accounts.
  • Opted in, not merely listed. Every tester has to open the opt-in link and then install from the Play Store listing. Being added to your tester list is not the same thing.
  • Fourteen continuous days. The window has to run unbroken, which means the opted-in count cannot dip below twelve at any point.
  • Drop-out replacement. People drift. A service that does not replace them is a service that lets your count fall and your window restart.
  • A written record. Opt-in timestamps, daily activity, devices and Android versions. Evidence, not a screenshot of a nice number.
  • Support while the test is live. The problems happen during the fortnight, so the answer has to arrive during the fortnight.

What happens step by step

  1. You create the closed track. In Play Console, under Testing, with your own account. Credentials never change hands.
  2. You share the opt-in link. The tester list is added to the track and the link is circulated.
  3. Testers opt in and install. Each one joins through the link and installs from Google Play.
  4. The window runs. Daily activity is tracked and inactive testers are replaced so the count holds at or above twelve.
  5. You receive the report. Opt-ins, activity, devices, and feedback, in a form you can keep.
  6. You apply for production access. With the testing evidence already documented.

Your app and account stay yours

No service should need your Play Console password or your signing key to run a closed test. You create the track, you hold the account, and testers only ever need the opt-in link. Anyone asking for more than that is asking for more than the job requires.

Where the money actually goes

A closed test that satisfies Google means 168 genuine sessions: twelve people, opening your app on fourteen separate days. That is the cost you are really paying, and it is why an honest price is not five dollars.

The rest buys the parts that stop the window breaking: vetting each tester before they join, watching the opt-in count daily, replacing anyone who goes quiet, and answering you while the campaign is live.

What a report should look like

Ask for these four things and keep them. If a production access decision ever goes against you, this is the material you would respond with.

  • Opt-in timestamps for each tester, which show when the window actually started.
  • Daily activity across the fourteen days, not a single install date.
  • The device models and Android versions involved.
  • What testers reported, and what you changed because of it.

What nobody should promise you

No service can guarantee the outcome of a Google review. Approval depends on your app, your listings, and your answers as well as your test. Treat a guaranteed approval as a warning sign rather than a feature.

Running it yourself instead

Everything on this page can be done without paying anyone. The requirement is the same either way: twelve opted-in testers, fourteen continuous days, and a record of what happened. If you have the network and the patience, do it yourself and skip the cost.

The reason people pay is the drop-out problem. Twelve friends is easy to gather on day one and hard to hold on day nine.
